# SPEC.md — Are we normalizing surveillance in the name of safety?

## Problem

I don’t know if this is just my imagination.<p>In recent years, I feel like the whole world is slowly moving toward this direction:<p>“Because we need to protect people, we need to monitor them.”<p>As a Taiwanese person, I’m very familiar with this kind of logic from the CCP next door.<p>But for some reason, I feel like more and more of the world is also moving in this direction.<p>For example:<p>* Asking an important open-source infrastructure project like Linux to add age verification.
* Credit card companies asking Japan to follow all kinds of unreasonable rules.
* Using courts to force companies, in the name of human rights or safety, to monitor cloud data.
* Requiring VPNs to be controlled or regulated.<p>These are just some simple examples. I don’t have detailed statistics, but I really feel like there have been more and more cases like this recently.<p>It is often wrapped in something morally justified, and then surveillance and control are introduced underneath it.<p>The CCP next door has always used this kind of logic.<p>Officially, everything is for protection and safety. Encryption is heavily restricted. Whenever these policies are introduced, there are also many people defending them.<p>Many people do not really feel that their own rights are being violated.<p>Until one day, the restrictions keep expanding and become something like a censorship system where almost anything can be punished.<p>On mainland Chinese platforms, if you try to have a deeper discussion about certain topics, or even just ask for opinions, it is very easy to get banned.<p>Right now, fortunately, there are still many people around the world who keep pushing back against different kinds of surveillance that are packaged as “morally necessary.”<p>But what I’m afraid of is this:<p>What if people slowly become used to it?<p>What if everyone becomes more and more insensitive to these things, like many people in mainland China, and only realizes what happened when almost nothing can be said anymore?<p>At that point, it may already be too late.<p>As a side note, the security and identity system in mainland China is basically built more from the perspective of the state.<p>So you can see many things like:<p>* A phone number is required almost everywhere.
* Email is not used very much.
* Communication is mainly through QQ or WeChat.
* Passkeys are not common. Most identity verification is based on phone numbers.
* Many services look anonymous, but are actually tied to your real identity.<p>A lot of the “security” mechanisms are related to the state, including speech and political control, rather than the personal security or privacy of the user.<p>Apps also like to put advertisements everywhere.<p>One app tries to do everything.<p>Many apps constantly encourage people to borrow money.<p>Even on phones and other hardware, anything that allows you to type text may potentially contain blocked words.<p>Some topics basically cannot be discussed.<p>Xi Jinping is one obvious example. Even related words or associations, such as “Xi” or Winnie the Pooh, can become sensitive.<p>China is also very strong in AI, partly because there is so much data and not much privacy.<p>Sometimes you can see Chinese AI systems that speak in a way that feels very similar to how real people think and talk.<p>My guess is that this is partly because a lot of real human-to-human conversations have been used for training, including private conversations.<p>There is probably much more I could add.<p>But the main point is not really China.<p>What I am worried about is that one day, even installing Linux on my own computer may require real-name verification.<p>That kind of thing sounds terrifying to me.<p>Maybe I’m overthinking it, but I really feel like the direction is becoming more obvious.<p>I’m curious what people here think.<p>Do you feel the same way?<p>Do you think this is actually becoming a broader trend?
