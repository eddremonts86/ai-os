# Designers are losing their bearings in the era of AI — it's unclear which direction to develop in, which stack to master, and how to become a «highly valuable specialist».

> Auto-generated product brief. Review and refine before MVP scoping.

## Value Proposition

A designer gets one trajectory instead of a sea of scattered advice: what to learn, in what order, practised in a simulator that scores visuals, working with AI and the ability to simplify complex scenarios — plus feedback on whether the direction they picked is holding. The author's stated alternative is courses that lag behind the models and teach button-pushing.

## Target Users

| Stakeholder | Why they care |
|---|---|
| Mid-to-senior product designer | Torn between options, time running out, paralysed by the fear of choosing wrong; the cost of the mistake is their profession. |
| Job-hunting designer | Needs demonstrable current capability, not a certificate from a course written before the last model shipped. |
| Designers watching big tech hiring | See narrow deep expertise being hired and traditional hiring dying, and cannot tell which narrow expertise to pick. |

## Jobs To Be Done

1. **Functional job** — Decide what specifically to learn to be in the minority still earning in a year, and be able to practise it.
2. **Emotional job** — Stop being paralysed by the fear of choosing the wrong direction.
3. **Social job** — Be the specialist who single-handedly delivers what used to take a studio, rather than one more designer copying everyone else.

## Success Metrics

- **Trajectory commitment:** share of users who pick a direction and are still working it a month later, versus the paralysis they arrive with.
- **Calibration movement:** measured change on the three axes the author names — visuals, working with AI, simplifying complex scenarios.
- **Agent-system output:** users who ship a working multi-agent workflow of their own, which is the concrete capability being taught.
- **Freshness lag:** days between a notable model or approach shipping and the simulator's scenarios reflecting it — the metric on which courses fail.

## Pricing & Monetization

Willingness to pay is asserted, the amount is not — treat the absence as signal, not an invitation to guess.

## Competitive Landscape

What the author tried and why each falls short:

- **Online courses** — always lag: new models and approaches release while the course is being written. They also teach you to "push buttons" rather than to design systems of agents.
- **Professional chats and colleague conversations (Yandex, T-Bank)** — real information, entirely unsystematic.
- **Following new LLMs and vibe coding directly** — hands-on, but no rubric and no feedback on whether the direction is right.
- **A sea of scattered blog posts and news** — the author's own description; volume without a system.

## Risks & Open Questions

- [ ] Nobody can actually verify which direction will "hit the mark" in a year. The product promises feedback on a bet whose outcome is unknowable, and overclaiming here would be the central dishonesty.
- [ ] Scoring a designer's visuals and their ability to simplify complex scenarios is judgement-heavy; a rubric that cannot be defended will not be trusted by the audience most sensitive to taste.
- [ ] The staleness problem the author uses to dismiss courses applies to this product too, and harder — simulator scenarios about agent orchestration age as fast as the frameworks.
- [ ] No price and no market data. One detailed account from a job-hunting designer in Russia is the entire evidence base.
