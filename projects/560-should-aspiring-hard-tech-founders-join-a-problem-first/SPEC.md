# Should aspiring hard-tech founders join a problem-first or technology-first PhD lab?

## Problem

I'm an engineering undergrad considering pursuing a PhD with the long-term goal of founding a hard-tech startup. I’m undecided about the specific area and have only a vague sense of what interests me (some areas that currently interest me are industrial decarbonization, mining / mineral processing, and advanced materials). However, I'm unsure which type of PhD lab would be better: a problem-first lab (e.g., like Yet-Ming Chiang's lab at MIT) or a technology-first lab (e.g., a typical academic lab that focuses on novel science without a predetermined application, and where commercialization happens largely by chance -- when a technology happens to have a valuable market application). I've looked at advice online from successful hard-tech entrepreneurs, but found conflicting answers. Some say to work backwards from a problem, while others argue that problem-first approaches often fail because it is difficult to force a scientific breakthrough around a predetermined problem.. Instead, they argue that most hard-tech companies are only founded because somebody made a scientific breakthrough and realized afterwards that there might be a commercial application. With that in mind, does anybody have any advice on which type of PhD lab to join? submitted by /u/misterballerdontlie [link] [comments]

---

## Objective

Frame the choice between problem-first labs (Yet-Ming Chiang's lab at MIT as the cited example) and technology-first labs so a prospective hard-tech founder can decide where to spend their PhD years based on their own risk profile and tolerance for commercialization uncertainty.

## Target Users

Engineering undergraduates considering a PhD with the long-term intent of founding a hard-tech startup. The author lists three interest areas — industrial decarbonization, mining/mineral processing, and advanced materials — and is choosing between labs whose work is anchored to a target problem and labs that pursue novel science with commercialization as a downstream byproduct.

## MVP Scope

A decision framework, not a product. Deliverable is a written comparison covering: (1) how each lab type structures the path from thesis to founding team, (2) what failure looks like for each path, and (3) what background signal each path produces for future co-founder or VC recruitment. The MVP of the framework is two case studies — one founder out of a problem-first lab, one out of a technology-first lab.

See `DESIGN.md` for this project's design tokens.

## Constraints

No primary research beyond public interviews and founder essays. Avoid generalising from a single anecdote. The author's interest areas (decarbonization, mineral processing, advanced materials) constrain the framework to hard-tech with a 5–15 year commercialization horizon, not software or biotech.
