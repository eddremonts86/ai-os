# I will not promote: did ai make it harder for real startups to win?

> Auto-generated product brief. Reviewed and refined for the post-AI quality-signal discovery tool.

## Value Proposition

The poster is asking whether AI made it harder for real, value-doing startups to win because the cost of building dropped and the noise floor rose. Cheap production flooded the space with "looks like a product but isn't solving anything", and the teams doing hard, valuable work get lost in it, or worse, get assumed to be API wrappers. This product is a curator with a published, transparent rubric that surfaces the real teams in a public directory; each entry is annotated with a short depth score the visitor can read in under a minute. The MVP is a browsable index, a small editorial workflow, and a weekly digest, so the curation is auditable rather than a black-box "we liked it".

## Target Users

- **Early-stage investor** — cares because they want a way to find teams doing real work under the AI wrapper, instead of having to surface that signal themselves in a saturated inbound pipeline.
- **B2B buyer or design partner** — cares because they have been burned by AI-wrapped products and want a vetted list before booking a demo.
- **Journalist or newsletter writer** covering the post-AI startup landscape — cares because the directory is a sourced list of "actually doing it" teams they can profile.
- **Solo founder of a real, deep startup** — cares because the directory is a discovery surface that does not lump them in with the weekend wrappers.
- **Curious peer in the r/startups tradition** — cares because the directory is the "front page of the actually-doing-it" feed they can watch.

## Jobs To Be Done

- **Functional:** When I want to find a real startup solving a real problem in a noisy AI-generated market, I want a curated, rubric-scored directory, so I can spend my time on the teams that have done the work, not the wrappers.
- **Emotional:** I want to stop feeling like every interesting-looking product is a thin wrapper, so I can trust my discovery again.
- **Social:** I want to be able to send a peer a link to a vetted top-N list instead of a generic "well, depends", so the conversation moves forward.

## Success Metrics

- Median time from curator intake to published entry — target under 7 days, demonstrating editorial pace.
- Number of newsletter subscribers who receive the weekly digest — target over 1,000 within three months; the digest is the discovery surface.
- Click-through rate from the weekly digest to the directory — target over 25%.
- Number of founders who embed the depth-score widget on their own site — target over 50, the social-proof signal.
- Self-reported rubric inter-rater reliability: when two outside curators apply the rubric to the same 20 entries, the scores agree within one band — target over 80%.

## Competitive Landscape

- **Product Hunt** is open submission and ranks by vote; it does not separate wrappers from real products.
- **Y Combinator's Launch YC** is a curated list, but only YC funded companies, and the curation criteria are not published.
- **Hacker News "Show HN"** surfaces interesting work but the bar is "interesting to HN readers", not "doing real work in a noisy market".
- **Crunchbase / PitchBook** are data dumps, not curated; the depth score is precisely the layer they do not have.

## Risks & Open Questions

- Will the rubric be perceived as gatekeeping by the founders who are rejected, and how does the curator respond without becoming a complaint department?
- Does "depth" map cleanly to what investors and buyers want, or is it the curator's taste dressed up as a metric?
- Will the directory become a target for founders trying to game the rubric, and how does the rubric evolve without becoming a moving target?
- Is the curator's identity the right thing to keep private, or would attaching a known editor's name improve trust?
- The poster's question is open-ended; the rubric answers it with a curation, not a research finding, and the product must be honest about that.
