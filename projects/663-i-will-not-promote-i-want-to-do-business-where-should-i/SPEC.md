# I will not promote: I want to do business. Where should I start?

## Problem
 Hi everyone, I want to do business. Although I have a business background, I still don't feel confident enough, and I feel like I don't have much practical knowledge about actually starting and running a business. I'm genuinely at the very beginning and haven't started anything yet. Before I begin this journey, I want to learn from people who have actually started businesses and understand how experienced founders think about starting one. Some questions I have: How do I know whether a business idea is actually worth pursuing? How do I research and validate an idea before putting significant time or money into it? How important is the idea itself? Is the idea the most important part, or are the customer, the problem being solved, product or service, execution, sales, distribution, or something else more important? As someone with a business background, can I start a business in a completely different field where I don't have an academic or professional background? When starting your first business, is it better to take an existing business idea or model that already works and create your own version, or should you try to come up with something genuinely unique? If you are building something that already exists, how different does it actually need to be? What are the biggest mistakes new founders commonly make? What books, courses, resources, or other things would you recommend learning before starting? I know its too many points but im just putting my thoughts into a post I'm genuinely at the very beginning and haven't started anything yet. I want to understand how experienced founders think about starting a business and what they prioritize before taking that first step. submitted by /u/Maximum-Page3433 [link] [comments]

## Objective

Convert the poster's broad "where should I start?" question into a structured first-30-days guide for a first-time founder who has a business education but no operating experience. The product is a static playbook plus a small "decision journal" web app that walks the founder through the seven questions the poster asked, in order, with a one-page artefact per question (idea score, problem interview log, distribution hypothesis, etc.). The deliverable is the playbook as a published read, and the journal as a private workspace where the founder's answers are time-stamped and versionable so the founder can re-run the exercise six months later. The MVP presupposes nothing about the founder's industry or skill set — the source provides no surface, so the tool is honest about being a generic founder-onboarding template, not a vertical-specific idea evaluator.

## Target Users

- **First-time founder with a business degree but no operating experience** — the poster's exact shape, looking for a checklist and a script rather than a manifesto.
- **Career-changer considering a first business** (corporate, consulting, agency) who has savings runway but no idea which direction to pick.
- **Solo founder-to-be with a list of half-formed ideas** who wants to score them against each other before committing.
- **University student or recent graduate** considering entrepreneurship as a first career move.
- **Small-business adviser or mentor** who runs a first-time-founder cohort and wants a structured workbook to hand each cohort member.

## MVP Scope

- A 30-day playbook published as a static site, with one task per day across the seven questions the poster asked.
- A decision journal web app where the founder logs daily notes (problem interviews, idea scores, distribution hypotheses) and tags each entry to one of the seven questions.
- An "idea scorecard" template: a 10-row scoring sheet the founder fills for each candidate idea, with a published rubric.
- A short prompt library for "talk to people with the problem" interviews, since the poster explicitly asks how to research.
- A weekly check-in email that asks the founder what they learned this week and surfaces it in the journal.
- A public, anonymised example journal from a real founder who has done the 30 days, so the playbook is not a blank page.

## Constraints

- The MVP does not pick an idea for the founder; the tool surfaces rubric scores and the founder decides.
- The source provides no specific industry, vertical, or product surface; the playbook is generic by design and the journal is the founder's canvas.
- The MVP does not include a marketplace, a community, or matchmaking with a mentor; the founder reads the playbook and keeps the journal alone.
- The MVP assumes the founder has at least 30 days of part-time availability; full-time founders are out of scope.
- The MVP does not promise outcomes — the playbook is a process, not a guarantee of success.
