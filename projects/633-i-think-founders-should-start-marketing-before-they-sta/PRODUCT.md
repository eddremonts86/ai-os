# I think founders should start marketing before they start building.

> Auto-generated product brief. Reviewed and refined for the pre-build marketing-validation framework.

## Value Proposition

The poster argues that finishing a product does not create demand, and that the right time to start marketing is before a single line of code is written: talk to people with the problem, post the problem publicly, watch what gets responses, and only then build the smallest version that solves it. This product packages that loop into a six-week protocol with a research journal, a weekly email checkpoint, and a hard build/pivot/kill decision at the end. The founder walks away with either evidence of demand sufficient to justify a six-month build, or a written kill list of problems that did not survive public scrutiny — and they get that answer in six weeks instead of six months of building in the dark.

## Target Users

- **Solo first-time SaaS founder with 6 months of runway** — cares because they cannot afford to spend three months coding something nobody asked for and still discover the problem is not real.
- **First-time technical founder leaving a corporate job** — cares because they have a salary runway and an idea but no audience and no script for the first conversation.
- **Indie hacker building a weekend MVP** — cares because they have a graveyard of domain registrations and want a process that forces one honest answer before the next build.
- **Non-technical domain expert with a vertical problem** — cares because they understand the problem space but not how to start a public conversation about it.
- **Founding team of 2-3 people** — cares because they need to align on the problem before splitting the work and burning team time on the wrong hypothesis.

## Jobs To Be Done

- **Functional:** When I have a startup idea I cannot shake, I want a six-week process that proves the problem is real before I code, so I can decide whether to build with confidence or kill the idea with receipts.
- **Emotional:** I want to stop feeling lonely during the build, the way the poster describes, so I can be in conversation with real people while the work is in progress instead of in a vacuum.
- **Social:** I want to be able to say publicly, with evidence, "I talked to 47 people with this problem before I built this", so my eventual launch lands as a researched answer and not a hope.

## Success Metrics

- Fraction of founders who reach a written build/pivot/kill decision within six weeks of signing up — target over 70%.
- Number of public posts and logged conversations per founder at the end of week 6 — target over 30.
- Fraction of founders who decide BUILD and report three months later that they are still building the same problem — target over 50% (the relevant signal is staying with the problem, not pivoting away).
- Self-reported confidence score (1-10) at week 0 vs week 6 — target improvement of 2 points.
- Open rate of the weekly email digest — target over 60%, because the digest is the forcing function.

## Competitive Landscape

- **The Mom Test / Rob Fitzpatrick's pre-build customer-development discipline** in book form is the closest philosophical cousin, but reading it does not produce a weekly log.
- **Crisp's Problem-Solution Fit canvas** is a static artifact; this product is six weeks long and forces a weekly publish.
- **Indie Hackers / Product Hunt Ship logs** are public, retrospective, and post-build; they do not give the founder a system to run pre-build.
- **Substack and ConvertKit** let a founder publish publicly, but neither pushes the weekly "did you post?" checkpoint the poster is describing.

## Risks & Open Questions

- What if the founder's six weeks produce no signal not because the problem is wrong but because the founder's first channel is wrong? The protocol would generate false kills.
- Will the weekly email digest feel like nagging to a founder who is already anxious? The tone has to be carefully calibrated so it is a coach, not a parent.
- Six weeks is arbitrary; some problems need twelve months of quiet observation. The MVP should not pretend the protocol is universal.
- The poster's own behaviour change ("i've changed my mind on this") suggests the protocol is mostly internal. The tool helps, but cannot force the founder to do the work.
- The "talk to people with the problem" stage is the hardest to scale or automate; the MVP must resist the temptation to substitute AI summarisation for real conversations.
