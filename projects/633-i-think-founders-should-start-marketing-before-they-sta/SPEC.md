# I think founders should start marketing before they start building.

## Problem
 i used to think marketing starts when the product is ready build the thing polish it launch it then figure out how to get people to use it i've changed my mind on this if nobody is interested in the problem before you build it then finishing the product doesn't magically create demand now i'd rather spend time: talking to people with the problem reading how they describe it posting about the problem watching what gets responses and seeing if anyone actually cares then build the smallest version that solves it it also makes building less lonely instead of spending 2 months wondering if anyone wants what you're making you get signals while you're building the hard part is that marketing this early doesn't feel like marketing it feels more like research curious how other founders approach this do you start marketing before your MVP is ready or wait until you have something people can actually use? submitted by /u/ryhanships [link] [comments]

## Objective

Turn the poster's pre-build marketing-as-research loop into a repeatable, time-boxed process that a solo founder can run before writing the first line of product code. The deliverable is a structured six-week protocol plus a lightweight tool that captures conversations, posts, and observed responses in one place, so that by the end of week six the founder has either qualitative evidence of demand strong enough to justify building, or a written kill list of problems that did not survive public scrutiny. The tool replaces the "open Notion, lose steam" failure mode with a weekly checkpoint that forces the founder to publish, listen, and decide.

## Target Users

- **Solo first-time SaaS founders** who have savings for ~6 months and want to prove the problem is worth their run before they invest months coding.
- **First-time technical founders leaving a corporate job** who have an idea but no existing audience and do not know how to start a public conversation.
- **Indie hackers shipping weekend MVPs** who keep building things nobody uses and want a forced upfront demand test.
- **Non-technical domain experts** (consultants, operators in a vertical) who have a problem hypothesis but no marketing background and need a script, not a framework document.
- **Small founding teams of 2-3 people** who want to align on "what problem are we actually building for" before splitting up the work.

## MVP Scope

- A six-week protocol document with weekly checkpoints: Week 1 (talk to 10 people), Week 2 (post the problem publicly), Week 3 (read and tag every response), Week 4 (rewrite your problem statement), Week 5 (re-publish), Week 6 (decide).
- A single-page "research journal" web app where the founder logs each conversation, post, and observed response, with structured fields (problem, whose, signal strength).
- A weekly email digest sent to the founder summarising what was logged, what was missing, and a hard prompt: "did you publish this week?".
- A kill-list template the founder fills at week 6 with the result: BUILD, PIVOT, or KILL, plus the reasoning captured from the journal.
- A small library of "talking to people with the problem" prompts the founder can copy, not invent.
- A public-facing link the founder can share when posting about the problem on Reddit, X, or LinkedIn, so responses land in the journal directly.

## Constraints

- The product itself is the protocol — there is no separate marketing tool, no CRM, no outreach sequencer. The tool is the journal.
- The MVP does not help build or ship the eventual product; it ends at the build/pivot/kill decision.
- No social listening, no scraping, no AI summarisation of strangers' posts. The founder logs signals manually; that friction is the point.
- The MVP is single-user; teams are out of scope.
- The MVP assumes the founder has at least one channel where they can post publicly (Reddit, X, LinkedIn, a niche forum) and refuses to be useful without one.
